Foundations

👻 Hallucinations & Grounding

Why LLMs confidently make things up — and how to prevent it.

👻 Core Concept

What It Is

A hallucination is when the model confidently generates plausible-sounding but false or fabricated information, stated with the same fluent tone as a correct answer.
The danger is precisely that it's indistinguishable in style from a true statement, there's no built-in hedge or wobble in the phrasing to warn you.
It can happen anywhere: facts, citations, code, math, or reasoning steps, wherever the model has to produce specific content rather than general prose.

The Root Cause

The model is a statistical next-token predictor trained to imitate confident-sounding answers in its training data, it was never directly optimized to say "I don't know."
It has no built-in mechanism to detect its own ignorance unless that behavior was explicitly taught during training, generating text is generating text whether the underlying fact is solid or shaky.
It cannot distinguish memorized fact from plausible-sounding pattern completion, a fabricated citation and a real one are constructed by the same next-token process and can look equally fluent.

Frequency & Reliability

Hallucination is worse for rare or obscure entities and better for frequently-discussed topics, because recall reliability tracks how often something appeared in training data.
This mirrors vague human memory: something you read a hundred times you recall with confidence and accuracy, something you skimmed once you might misremember while still feeling sure about it.
A well-known public figure's biography is usually solid; an obscure local figure or a niche internal document is exactly where fabrication risk spikes.
🔬 Deeper Look

Hallucination Patterns

Fabricated citations, URLs, and statistics: plausible-looking paper titles, broken links, and invented numbers presented with full confidence.
Invented API methods or library functions: code that calls a function that sounds right but doesn't exist, generated by pattern-completing "what a real API in this style would look like."
Confident wrong answers to "who is X" for obscure people, filling in a biography-shaped answer even when the model has almost no real signal about the person.
Made-up details completing a plausible structure, like a book ISBN or a serial number, where the model fills the expected slot with something structurally valid but fictitious.

Grounding As The General Principle

The more directly relevant information is present in the context window, the less the model needs to rely on fuzzy internal recollection to answer.
Grounding shifts the task from "recall a fact from training" to "read and report a fact that's sitting right in front of you," a much easier and more reliable operation.
Every mitigation technique below is really a variation on this one idea: reduce reliance on parametric memory, increase reliance on verifiable, in-context source material.

Teaching Refusal

One direct mitigation is interrogating the model during data prep to find its actual knowledge boundary, what it reliably knows versus what it's shaky on.
Then adding "I don't know" examples to training data for exactly those shaky areas, so the model learns to express uncertainty tied to an internal confidence signal rather than always answering.
Done well, this behavior generalizes: the model learns the pattern of "when internally uncertain, hedge or decline" rather than memorizing a fixed list of unknowns.
🛠️ Mitigation Techniques

Retrieval & Grounding

RAG (retrieval-augmented generation): give the model the actual source text in context so it's referencing working memory instead of vague parametric recollection.
This directly attacks the root cause: the model doesn't need to recall a fact from training when the fact is right there in the prompt to read and summarize.
Quality of retrieval matters, if the wrong passage is retrieved, the model can still hallucinate confidently based on irrelevant grounding, so this is not a silver bullet on its own.

Tools & Verification

Tool use like web search lets the model fetch current, verifiable information instead of relying on a frozen training snapshot.
Requiring citations and verifying them programmatically (checking a URL resolves, a quote actually appears in the source) catches fabrications before they reach the user.
Structured output validation: checking generated IDs, dates, or schema fields against a real system of record rather than trusting the model's output at face value.

Generation-Level Controls

Lower temperature for factual tasks, reducing the randomness that can push the model off the highest-probability, most-supported continuation.
These controls reduce hallucination rate but don't eliminate it, a low-temperature model can still confidently state something false if that's what its training distribution favors.
Combining several mitigations (grounding plus verification plus lower temperature) compounds the effect better than relying on any single one.
🎯 Applying It

Where Risk Is Highest

Open-ended factual Q&A with no supporting context, the model has nothing to ground against but its own training recall.
Code generation against unfamiliar or fast-moving libraries, where the model's training data may be stale or thin.
Long-form generation where a plausible narrative structure invites the model to fill in specific, unverifiable details to keep the structure coherent.

Where Risk Is Lower

Tasks that are grounded in provided context, like summarizing a document that's directly in the prompt.
Well-established, frequently-discussed topics that appeared heavily and consistently across training data.
Constrained generation tasks like classification or extraction against a fixed schema, where there's less room to fabricate freeform content.

Reliability

Hallucination is fundamentally never fully solved, only mitigated, any system that presents LLM output as fact needs a human or programmatic check somewhere in the loop for high-stakes claims.
Treat model output as a draft or a hypothesis in high-stakes domains (medical, legal, financial), not as ground truth, regardless of how confident the phrasing sounds.
Mitigate with grounding via RAG, teaching explicit refusal for low-confidence areas, tool use for verifiable current facts, citation requirements with automated verification, structured output validation, and lower temperature for factual tasks, layered together rather than relying on any single fix.
