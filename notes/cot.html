Foundations

🔗 Chain-of-Thought (CoT)

Making LLMs reason step by step to improve accuracy on complex tasks.

🔗 Core Concept

What It Is

Asking the model to reason step by step before giving a final answer, instead of jumping straight to a conclusion.
Two common ways to invoke it: zero-shot ("think step by step" or "explain your reasoning first"), or few-shot, showing worked examples that include the reasoning steps, not just the final answer.
Either way, the model produces visible intermediate steps that lead up to the final answer, rather than emitting the answer in one shot.

Why It Improves Accuracy

The core mechanical reason: each output token gets roughly a fixed amount of compute during generation.
Forced to answer a hard multi-step problem in a single token, the model has to effectively guess, there's no room to actually work through the steps.
Spreading reasoning across many tokens lets the model do the computation incrementally, each individual step being easy on its own, with the running text itself acting as working memory the model can condition on.
This is why CoT is a mechanical unlock, not just a stylistic preference, more tokens genuinely means more effective compute applied to the problem.

Where It Helps Most

Big gains on math, logic, and code: multi-step problems where an intermediate error compounds if skipped, and where the final answer genuinely depends on getting several steps right in sequence.
Little to no benefit on simple factual lookups: "what is the capital of France" doesn't get more accurate by reasoning about it, the answer is a direct retrieval, not a derivation.
The dividing line is whether the task requires composing multiple intermediate results, if yes, CoT tends to help a lot; if it's single-hop recall, it mostly just adds latency.
🔬 Deeper Look

Prompted CoT vs Trained Reasoning Models

Prompted CoT: works on any base instruction-tuned model, just by asking it to think step by step or showing worked examples, no special training needed.
Trained/native reasoning models (o1/o3, DeepSeek-R1, Claude extended thinking): specifically trained via reinforcement learning to produce long internal reasoning chains before answering.
These models discover strategies like backtracking, self-correction, and "wait, let me reconsider" as an emergent property of that RL training, not because anyone hand-wrote those behaviors into a prompt.
Prompted CoT nudges an existing model toward reasoning behavior; trained reasoning models have that behavior optimized directly into how they generate.

Self-Consistency

Sample multiple independent CoT reasoning paths for the same question, each arriving at its own final answer.
Take a majority vote across the final answers, rather than trusting any single reasoning chain.
This further boosts accuracy because different sampled paths make different mistakes, and correct reasoning tends to converge on the same answer more often than any one specific error does.

Cost & Latency Trade-off

More reasoning tokens means slower responses and higher cost, since output tokens are billed and generated sequentially.
Not worth it for trivial queries: forcing a reasoning model to "think" about a simple lookup just burns latency and money for no accuracy gain.
Production systems often route: cheap fast models or no-CoT for simple queries, reasoning models or explicit CoT prompting reserved for genuinely hard multi-step requests.
👁️ Visible vs Hidden Reasoning

Visible Reasoning

Some providers show the full raw chain of thought to the user, useful for debugging, trust, and understanding how the model reached an answer.
Downside: raw reasoning can be messy, long, or contain false starts that look alarming out of context even when the final answer is correct.

Hidden Reasoning Traces

Other providers hide the raw chain of thought and show only a cleaned-up summary of the reasoning to the end user.
Partly a UX choice (raw traces are noisy), and partly to prevent distillation, competitors training their own models by imitating the visible reasoning strategy of a stronger model.
This is an active area of provider-level policy, not a fixed technical constraint, the same underlying reasoning tokens exist either way.

Where It Fits

Prompt, then Reasoning Tokens (visible or hidden), then Final Answer Extraction, then Response to User.
CoT sits between prompting and generation, it doesn't change what the model knows, it changes how much intermediate work it's allowed to show before committing to an answer.
🎯 Applying It

Use Cases

Multi-step math and word problems where skipping steps compounds error.
Code generation and debugging, especially when the fix depends on tracing through logic first.
Complex multi-constraint planning, like scheduling or multi-hop question answering that requires combining several facts.
Agentic tool-use decisions, where the model benefits from reasoning about which tool to call and why before acting.

Trade-offs

Latency and cost scale with reasoning length, sometimes several times the cost of a direct answer.
Not a guarantee of correctness, a confident-sounding chain of reasoning can still reach a wrong conclusion, fluency is not the same as validity.
Verbosity can bury the actual answer in a wall of intermediate text if not managed with format constraints.

When NOT to Use

Simple factual or lookup queries where reasoning adds latency with no accuracy benefit.
Extremely latency-sensitive paths (autocomplete, real-time UI) where a slower, more thoughtful answer isn't worth the wait.
High-volume low-stakes classification, where a direct answer is cheap, fast, and already reliable enough.
Match reasoning effort to task difficulty: use direct answers for simple queries, CoT prompting for moderately complex ones, and full reasoning models only when the task genuinely requires multi-step deduction.
